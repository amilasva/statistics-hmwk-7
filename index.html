01 / MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS

Understanding independence

Theory

We observe two categorical variables, X and F. The central question is whether knowing the value of X changes what we can predict about F.

A · PROBABILITY

Independence of random variables

For every possible pair of categories, the joint probability equals the product of the marginal probabilities.

P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) × P(F = f)

Equivalently, when P(X = x) > 0, knowing X does not change the probability of F:

P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f)
B · STATISTICS

Samples are not populations

Even if variables are independent in the population, sample counts may differ from their expected values because of random sampling.

H₀: X ⟂ F   |   H₁: X and F are associated

The chi-square test evaluates how compatible our observed sample is with the independence hypothesis; it cannot prove independence.

From raw data to probability

3

Within one category of X, the proportion falling in category j of F.

ILLUSTRATION

Why conditioning matters

Suppose 60 students are observed. Of 30 group learners, 24 pass; of 30 solo learners, 12 pass. The overall pass rate is 36/60 = 60%, but the pass rate is 80% among group learners and 40% among solo learners.

80% ≠ 40%: in this sample, exam outcome depends on which study-method group we examine. This is descriptive association, not proof that studying in groups causes success.

How the test works

Step 1: Under H₀, calculate each expected count Eᵢⱼ = (row total × column total) / N.

Step 2: For every cell, calculate (Oᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ and add the contributions.

Step 3: Compare the statistic to a chi-square distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom. A small p-value means the sample would be unusual if H₀ were true.

06 / INTERPRETATION

What does independence mean?

A decision about a hypothesis is not the same as a statement about cause and effect. The strength and meaning of evidence depend on the data and sampling process.

In probability

Two events are independent when knowing one does not change the probability of the other: P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x)P(F = f) for every pair.

In observed data

Independence means the distribution of F is the same across the categories of X. Small differences can arise through random sampling.

Association ≠ causation

A statistically significant association does not establish that X causes F. Confounding variables, selection bias, or reverse causality may explain a relationship.
